Overview

What is an effect size?

An effect size is a standardised, unit-free measure of how big an effect is — independent of how many participants you collected. It answers the question that a p-value cannot: not just is there an effect?, but how meaningful is it?

There is no single effect size statistic. Which one you use depends on the kind of research question you are asking. There are three main families, each asking the "how big?" question in a different way:

Today we focus on mean difference effect sizes — specifically Cohen's d and its variants — because this is what you need for the experimental two-group designs at the heart of most lab studies. When you read a study and want to replicate it, d is typically what you are working from.

A quick primer on p-values, before we go further

Since we haven't dicsussed p-values in my earlier websites, a brief reminder. A p-value below .05 means the observed mean difference sits in the tail of the null probability distribution that is less likely than 1-in-20 if the true effect were exactly zero. The threshold of .05 has no deep theoretical meaning — it is a long-standing convention. What matters far more is what determines whether you cross it.

Throughout this page two ingredients will keep returning, because together they explain why any result is significant or not. The size of the effect — how big is the difference between conditions? — and the precision of the estimate — how certain are we about that difference, given how much data we have? Put simply: if a difference is very large, we can tolerate quite a lot of uncertainty about its exact value and still be confident it is not exactly zero (since this is all null hypothesis testing is actually asking). If the difference is small, we need to be much more certain about it — much more precise — before we can rule zero out. Both ingredients clearly contribute, and a p-value collapses them into a single number — which is exactly why a p-value alone can mislead.

Why effect size and precision both matter A tiny effect can produce p < .001 with a large enough sample. A genuinely large effect can fail to reach significance with a small enough sample. Effect size and precision are the two ingredients that together tell you what is going on. The rest of this page unpacks them in turn, then puts them back together.
Section 1

Why effect size? The problem with p < .05

The p-value tells you the probability of observing a result as extreme as yours, assuming the null hypothesis is true. Cross the threshold of .05 and researchers declare "significance." But as I just flagged, "significance" is the joint result of two ingredients — the size of the effect, and the precision with which it is estimated. The widget below makes that joint dependence visible.

Cohen's benchmarks — use with caution Before we use d in the widget, you need rough labels for what counts as small, medium, and large. Jacob Cohen (1988) offered: d = 0.2 (small), d = 0.5 (medium), d = 0.8 (large). These are starting points for intuition, not universal standards. A d of 0.2 might be negligible in a lab reaction time study but clinically important in a drug trial where the outcome is mortality. Always interpret effect size in context. We unpack what d actually is in §2.

The widget lets you set both the true effect size and the per-group sample size. Try the three reference patterns below, and watch how the same N can be perfectly adequate for one effect size and hopelessly underpowered for another:

  • d = 0.8 (large) and N small. Even with very few participants, a large effect can clear significance. The size carries the result.
  • d = 0.2 (small) and N small. Same N, very different verdict. The effect is real, but you do not have enough data to pin it down precisely enough to rule out zero.
  • d = 0.2 (small) and N large. Significance returns — but only because more data has bought more precision. The effect itself has not changed.

The takeaway: a p-value sitting just below .05 does not tell you the effect is large, and a p-value above .05 does not tell you the effect is absent. It tells you whether the combination of effect size and precision was enough to clear the threshold. A trivial effect plus a huge sample can do it. A real, substantial effect plus a small sample often cannot. Significance is a joint statement about the size of the phenomenon and the size of your study — and the rest of this page is about teasing those two apart.

Section 2

Computing Cohen's d

Cohen's d expresses the mean difference between two groups in standard deviation units. It is structurally identical to a z-score — instead of asking "how many SDs above the mean is this single observation?", we ask "how many SDs apart are these two group means?"

d = (M₁ − M₂) / SDpooled

The result is unit-free. Whether you measured reaction times in milliseconds or exam scores as percentages, d = 0.5 means the same thing: the two group means are half a standard deviation apart. This makes effects comparable across different studies and different measurement scales.

A natural way to visualise d is as two overlapping distributions. When d = 0 the groups are completely indistinguishable. As d increases, the distributions separate and the overlap shrinks.

At d = 1.0, 84% of one group scores below the average of the other — a substantial separation. At d = 0.2, only 58% do — barely better than chance. You can play with the key parameters (size difference between means/pooled variance) in the widget above to see the degree of this overlap - if you can create what a effect size of 0.2 looks like, it will be obvious how much the two distributions actually overlap in practice!

Two things to notice in the calculator that connect to the wider story of this page. First, the calculator uses a single pooled SD — it implicitly assumes both groups have the same within-group spread. As you drag the SD slider down, the two distributions sharpen, the overlap shrinks, and d grows. That is the precision side of the joint story showing up directly: lower within-group variability means each group's mean is estimated more sharply, so the same raw mean difference translates into a larger d. Higher SD means the same raw difference looks smaller in standardised units.

Second, Cohen's d is mechanical. The formula will compute a number whether or not the two groups actually share the same variance — and it will produce that number regardless of whether the homogeneity-of-variance assumption (which we discussed at length in Part 2 §01) holds. The calculator does not check, and neither does Cohen's d itself. If your two groups have genuinely different SDs, the choice of which SD to put in the denominator is no longer obvious, and d will give different answers depending on how you decide. That is the question §3 takes up.

Section 3

The denominator problem — all are compromises

Cohen's d divides the raw mean difference by a standard deviation. But which standard deviation? This turns out to be a genuine methodological question with no single correct answer. Different choices produce different variants of d, each making different assumptions about what the "reference" variability in your population should be.

The honest conclusion: all are defensible compromises. The right choice depends on your data and your assumptions — and the differences between them are smallest when the two group SDs are similar.

Practical guidance For most lab replication studies with random assignment, Cohen's d (pooled SD) is the standard default and what you will typically see reported. Glass's Δ is justified when you have a genuine untreated baseline group and believe the treatment may have altered score variability. Hedges' g is preferred with small samples (n < 20 per group) because it corrects for the slight upward bias in small-sample SD estimates. With reasonable sample sizes all three converge.
Section 4

The replication bridge — from effect size to sample size

You have read a published study. It reports Cohen's d = 0.5 for a two-group comparison. Your task is to replicate it. The critical question: how many participants do you need?

The bridge from d back to N runs through the standard error. We unpacked the difference between the standard deviation and the standard error in Part 1 in detail; the very short recap is that SD is a fixed property of the population (how much individual scores vary), while SE is a property of your estimate (how precisely your sample mean tracks the true population mean), and the link between them is:

SE = SD / √N

This single formula does most of the heavy lifting on this page. Three things hang off it that are worth holding together explicitly:

Connection back to §1 — why p depends on N

The test statistic (t or z) divides the raw mean difference by the SE. As N increases, SE shrinks, so the same true effect d produces a larger and larger test statistic and a smaller p-value. That is exactly the dynamic the §1 widget displayed. The "p depends on N" pattern is just SE = SD / √N showing up in the denominator of the test.

Connection forward to §5 — why N scales with d²

Because SE shrinks with √N (not N), each additional participant buys less precision than the last — the classic diminishing returns. Doubling N only multiplies precision by √2 ≈ 1.41, not by 2. To halve the SE you need to quadruple the sample. That is where §5's "N scales with d²" rule comes from.

So SE/√N is the same engine running in three places: it's why a tiny effect can become "significant" if N grows large enough (§1), it's how power analysis converts an expected d into a required N (§5), and — as we will see in the advanced section — it is also what makes the conventional null hypothesis structurally indefensible in the limit, since the SE will eventually shrink enough to produce p < .05 for any nonzero true effect.

Section 5

Power analysis — reverse engineering your sample size

Power analysis works backwards from the question: given the effect I expect to find, how many participants do I need to detect it reliably? The same two-ingredients message returns one more time, with the planning logic on top: the bigger you expect the effect to be, the less precision (and so the fewer participants) you will need to be confident the result clears "not zero". The smaller the expected effect, the more precision you will need, and the more participants this implies. Power analysis just makes that trade-off quantitative.

You supply three inputs and the analysis outputs the required N.

What "desired power" actually means α is the false-positive rate students usually have a feel for: ".05" means we accept a 1-in-20 chance of declaring an effect when there isn't one. Power (1 − β) is the matching question on the other side: given that the effect is real, what is the probability our study actually detects it? The conventional 80% means we are accepting a 1-in-5 chance of missing a real effect. This is the "certainty" half of the two ingredients made explicit at the planning stage. Higher desired power → more precision required → larger N. Lower power → smaller N but more frequent false negatives.

The underlying logic is geometric. You have two overlapping distributions of your test statistic. The null distribution (blue) is centred on zero — the "no effect" world. The alternative distribution (orange) is shifted by the true effect. Power (green shaded area) is the proportion of the alternative distribution that clears your critical value. Increasing N squeezes both distributions tighter, separating them. Power analysis finds the N at which the overlap drops to your acceptable threshold.

The N-scaling rule — commit this to memory Required N scales with the square of the effect size. Halve d → roughly quadruple N. This is why overestimating the effect size from a small pilot study is dangerous: the original estimate may be inflated by sampling error, and your replication will be designed for an effect that was over-optimistic. When replicating, consider using 90% power rather than 80%, or apply a modest downward correction to the published effect size to account for publication bias.

Try the calculator: set d = 0.8 (large) and compare the N to d = 0.4 (medium). Then compare d = 0.4 to d = 0.2 (small). The quadrupling effect is dramatic — and explains why so many underpowered studies in the literature have failed to replicate.

Advanced Section

The AnCred framework — credibility beyond p < .05

The central problem

With a point null hypothesis — the conventional assumption that the true effect is exactly zero — statistical significance is ultimately just a function of how long you are willing to collect data. Because SE shrinks with N, the null and alternative distributions will always eventually separate, for any true effect however trivial. Run enough participants and even a meaningless difference becomes "statistically significant."

The fix is to set the hypothesis being tested to a distribution of effects rather than a single point — a distribution that represents what a sceptic would have to believe before being convinced something real is going on. We will see two ways of operationalising this idea, plus a third framework that looks similar but works very differently. Untangling the three is the main business of this section.

Why the point null is a straw man

The point null says: "the true effect is precisely zero." But in the real world, no two groups are ever exactly identical. Any manipulation, any group difference, any intervention will move the mean by some amount — if only by a fraction. The question is never really is the effect zero? It is is the effect large enough to matter?

Because SE = SD / √N, increasing N always shrinks the SE, which always squeezes the null and alternative distributions further apart. This means any true effect — even one too small to care about — will produce p < .05 with a sufficiently large sample. Significance becomes a measure of your patience, not the importance of your finding.

Psychologist Paul Meehl identified this concretely with his concept of the crud factor: in psychology, almost any two groups you compare will differ on almost any measure you take, typically producing Cohen's d values around 0.2, purely because of the accumulated background correlation between all measured variables. A d of 0.2 is ambient noise — routinely observable, rarely meaningful. Yet with N = 200 per group it will produce p < .001. The point null has nothing useful to say about this.

The null-stretching solution Instead of asking "does the effect differ from precisely zero?", ask "does the effect clear a region around zero that represents either (a) what a reasonable sceptic should be willing to concede is plausible, or (b) the minimum size we'd consider practically meaningful?" Both reframings force significance to mean something real rather than just reflecting accumulated N.

Why does this happen?

The root cause is the same SE = SD / √N machinery we kept revisiting in §4 and built up properly in Part 1. As N grows, SE shrinks; the t-statistic — mean difference / SE — therefore grows toward infinity for any nonzero true effect, no matter how small.

t = mean difference / SE   =   mean difference / (SD / √N)

The point worth restating here is that this is not a bug in the statistics. The estimator is doing exactly what it should — becoming more and more precise. The bug is in the interpretation: treating "the t-statistic cleared the threshold" as equivalent to "the effect is meaningfully large." Those are completely different claims, and given enough patience the threshold will be cleared for any nonzero true effect, including effects too small to care about, like the 'background noise' of small correlations that Meehl suggests happen all the time. So the threshold itself stops being informative.

Three frameworks — three different ways to set up the question

Three different fixes have been proposed for this. They are easy to confuse because all three sound, on the surface, like they are "doing something with zero". Mechanically they are very different — in places, opposite. The cards below pull them apart.

1 · NHST (the default)
Point null at exactly zero

Tests whether the effect differs from exactly zero. Because the null is a single point, even a tiny nonzero effect rejects it given enough N — as we have just seen. This is the framework's structural weakness - all you are doing is chasing a binary outcome (i.e., below or above an arbitrary probability value).

2 · Reverse Bayes / AnCred
Tight distribution around zero

The hypothesis is now a distribution — typically Gaussian, centred on zero, deliberately tight. To shift it requires the data to be both large in effect and precise in CI. Larger effects are required to overturn tighter scepticism, where scepticism in this case is represented as higher and higher certainty that no effect actually exists.

3 · ROPE / Meehl's crud
Pre-specified equivalence region

Not really about zero at all. Decide in advance that anything below some threshold (e.g., d < 0.2 is "crud") doesn't count, then test whether the CI clears that boundary. Closer in spirit to NHST, but with a distribution as the threshold you are seeking to eliminate rather than exactly zero.

Why these are not the same idea — read this carefully In NHST, a tight focus on zero is structurally weak: any tiny nonzero effect will reject it given enough precision. In Reverse Bayes, a tight prior around zero is structurally strong — it carries informative content and resists being moved unless the data are both large and precise. Put simply if I have a very high certainty that the real effect is zero (I am VERY sceptical!) you will need to show me something extraordinary for me to think twice. The two frameworks operationalise "tight around zero" in opposite ways. ROPE is a third thing entirely: it does not centre on zero at all; it has chosen a non-trivial threshold above which an effect counts as real. So all three appear, on first hearing, to be "fixing the null", and all three are doing structurally different things.

The Scepticism Limit (SL) — how Reverse Bayes works

AnCred (Analysis of Credibility), developed by statistician Robert Matthews, computes the SL directly from your confidence interval. The sceptic maintains a prior distribution centred on zero — they are specifically doubtful that any effect exists. The SL answers: how wide does that distribution need to be before your data can overcome their scepticism?

The Bayesian mechanics under the hood

In a Bayesian update, posterior belief is a compromise between prior information (what was believed beforehand) and data information (how precise and strong the observed evidence is). In the normal–normal setting that maps cleanly to standard t-tests, the posterior mean is a weighted average:

μpost = w · μdata + (1 − w) · μprior

where the weight w increases as the data become more precise. A tight sceptical prior centred on zero — high certainty, low variance — operationalises the position: "I am fairly certain there is no meaningful effect unless compelling evidence says otherwise." Weak or noisy data won't shift such a prior. Only strong, precise data — large effect and tight CI — can move it appreciably.

Reverse Bayes inverts the operation. Rather than asserting a prior up front and computing a posterior, AnCred asks: how tight a sceptical prior would the observed data have just barely overturned? A low SL means even very tight scepticism cannot survive the evidence. A high SL means only loose, easily-shifted scepticism could be persuaded — which ironically is also the same as saying the evidence is not really compelling!

This is the place students often get confused, because NHST also "centres on zero". The key contrast: in NHST, "centred on zero" means a single point, which any tiny effect can reject given enough N. In Reverse Bayes, "centred on zero" means a tight distribution with informative content, which actively resists being moved. A wide sceptical prior is paradoxically a weak form of scepticism — a sceptic who concedes that effects up to 40 points are possible is easy to convince. The genuinely demanding sceptic holds a tight prior around zero. A low SL means your data defeated that hard opponent.

SL = (U − L)² / (4 × √(U × L)) where U and L are the upper and lower CI bounds — both must be positive, i.e. the result must be significant with both bounds on the same side of zero

The SL is expressed in your outcome's own units — points, milliseconds, whatever you measured — so you can immediately judge whether the implied sceptical region is defensible in your field. This is the key advantage over a p-value, which tells you nothing about the scale of doubt involved.

The verdict rule is simple:

Observed effect > SL → Credible Your effect sits outside the sceptic's zone. They cannot accommodate your result without widening their prior to an indefensible degree.
Observed effect < SL → Fragile Your effect sits inside the sceptic's zone. Even a sceptic centred on zero can comfortably absorb your result as "essentially no effect" — regardless of the p-value.
A caveat about the CI itself. The SL formula treats the observed CI as a fixed object, but as we discussed in Part 1 §03, a 95% CI is a property of the procedure (it contains the true parameter 95% of the time across hypothetical repetitions), not a property of the particular interval you have in your hands. The CI you observed is itself a sample-bound estimate. So SL is best read as "roughly how strong does scepticism need to be to absorb this result?" — a heuristic for credibility — rather than as a precise dividing line.

The Advocacy Limit (AL) — for non-significant results

When your result is non-significant — the CI straddles zero — the SL cannot be computed. But there is an equally important question for that situation: just because you didn't reach significance, does that mean the effect is absent?

The Advocacy Limit answers: what is the maximum effect size that even a committed advocate of the hypothesis can claim the data supports? A large AL means the study was simply too weak to say much either way — the absence of significance is not evidence of absence.

The most instructive example is the ORBITA trial, which tested whether stenting improved exercise duration in patients with stable angina. The result was non-significant (p ≈ .2), and many commentators concluded that stents don't work. But the AL was approximately 115 seconds — meaning the data were perfectly consistent with a clinically meaningful positive effect. The trial was underpowered, not decisive. The appropriate conclusion was "we don't yet know," not "no effect." This maps directly onto the small-N paradox in power analysis: a non-significant result from an underpowered study is an uninformative result, not evidence of zero effect. The AL makes that explicit and quantitative.

AL in plain language When your study is non-significant, ask: is the upper bound of my CI clinically or practically meaningful? If the CI runs from −2 to +40 points, you have not shown the effect is zero — you have shown the data are consistent with effects from trivial to substantial. A large AL is a call for a better-powered study, not a declaration of null results.

A note on what "prior" means here

The SL framework uses the word prior differently from classical Bayesian analysis.

Bayesian epistemic prior Encodes your genuine belief state before seeing the data. A flat prior expresses ignorance — "I have no idea what to expect." Appropriate when you genuinely lack prior knowledge. A statement about your knowledge state.
SL sceptical prior Models a specific argumentative position: "I doubt an effect exists." A tight prior around zero is not ignorance — it is an informative claim. The SL asks what the sceptic must concede as possible, even while remaining doubtful. A dialectical tool, not an epistemic one.

Counterintuitively, a flat prior is easy to defeat — it already spreads probability across large effects. The genuinely hard sceptic holds a tight prior around zero. A low SL means your data defeated that hard opponent. And this clarifies why NHST's point null is a false form of scepticism: it looks maximally demanding (all mass at exactly zero) but is trivially defeated with large N. The SL models scepticism as a region — which is how real scientific doubt actually operates.

How to use the three frameworks together
The three approaches we have separated out are not in competition — they answer different questions and can sit alongside each other.
  • ROPE / Meehl's crud works prospectively: before you collect data, decide what "negligible" means (e.g., d < 0.2 is just crud), and ask whether your effect clears that pre-specified threshold. This is the planning-side fix.
  • Reverse Bayes / SL works retrospectively: after the data are in, ask how tight a sceptical distribution your data can overturn. This is the credibility-side fix, evaluating what you already have.
  • NHST remains the conventional reporting framework, but with the structural caveat now spelled out: clearing p < .05 only tells you the true effect is not exactly zero, which (given enough N) is essentially guaranteed. The other two frameworks tell you something more useful.

A result that survives both ROPE and SL is genuinely robust. A result that survives only one deserves scrutiny.
