Reliability and the ICC — what they actually measure

We have 20 people, 5 video clips each, and 47 raters scoring perceived friendliness on a 1–9 scale. Before we can say anything useful about who appears more or less friendly, we need to ask: how much of a raw rating actually reflects the person being rated?

1 — The study and the question

The study asked 47 raters to score video clips of 20 people on perceived friendliness, using a scale from 1 (not at all friendly) to 9 (extremely friendly). Each of the 20 people was represented by 5 video clips, giving 100 clips in total. Raters scored all 100 clips across two sessions — so each rater produced 200 ratings in total, and each of the 100 clips received 47 ratings per session (94 across the two sessions).

The goal is to recover a stable rank ordering of the 20 people by perceived friendliness — not to measure the absolute level, but to say with confidence that Person A is seen as friendlier than Person B, who is friendlier than Person C, and so on. This rank ordering is what we would then want to use as a measure in subsequent research — for example, correlating perceived friendliness with personality traits, social outcomes, or cognitive variables.

The central question is: how much can we trust that rank ordering? If we repeated the study with a different set of 47 raters, or a different set of video clips of the same people, would we get the same ordering? Or is a rating of perceived friendliness so noisy and idiosyncratic that the ordering would look quite different each time?

The reliability question Reliability, in the psychometric sense used here, is not about whether our ratings are "correct" in some absolute sense. It is about consistency: does the measurement instrument — in this case, our rating procedure — produce the same answer when applied to the same thing more than once? A rank ordering that is stable across raters, video clips, and occasions is a reliable one.

2 — Signal and noise in a single rating

Consider what happens when a single rater looks at a single video clip and gives a friendliness score. Their rating will reflect several things simultaneously: the stable cues that make the person appear friendly (the signal we want), their own idiosyncratic preferences and standards (some raters are more generous than others), features of that particular video clip (a slightly awkward angle, an unusually bright smile), and moment-to-moment variation in attention and judgment.

All of these contribute to the number the rater writes down. Only one source of the observed variance — the person's stable perceived friendliness, the part that comes through consistently across clips and raters — is what we actually want to measure. Everything else is noise: real, systematic, but irrelevant to our research question.

We can see this across the whole stimulus set. In the figure below, the 100 clips are ordered left-to-right by their consensus score — the 47-rater average, the bold line — with three individual raters' profiles overlaid. The consensus climbs smoothly: that steady rise is the between-clip variance, the signal. But each rater's line zig-zags by several points from clip to clip, and sits at its own overall height. The zig-zag — disagreement about which clip outranks which — is the within-clip noise that matters for recovering an ordering; the differences in overall height (some raters simply more generous than others) we set aside shortly, because they leave the ordering intact. So a single rater's score for any one clip is an unstable estimate — and a stable, representative summary is exactly what we need. Every downstream use of these data depends on each aggregate — each clip's score, and ultimately each person's — faithfully representing what we are trying to measure. Making those aggregates as stable and representative as possible is the entire point of reliability, and it is what the rest of this series is about.

The split that defines reliability. Every rating's variance is between-clip (signal) plus within-clip (noise) — push the within part to zero and all raters agree perfectly, so a summary based on them is stable and meaningful; push the between part to zero and there is nothing to recover, so any summary is unstable and meaningless. Real data sits in between, and the ICC in Section 3 is simply the between share: signal ÷ (signal + noise). Everything after it — averaging raters, the mixed model, the design question in Part 4 — works by shrinking the within part to enlarge that share.

3 — Formalising the ratio — the ICC

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) puts a single number on exactly the split we have just been looking at. It is computed from an ANOVA decomposition: so following our earlier example, we can take the 100 video clips as the unit, and partition the total variance in ratings into a between-clip component (the signal) and everything else (the noise).

ICC(C,1) = MSBetween − MSWithin MSBetween + (k − 1) × MSWithin

k = number of raters  ·  MSBetween = between-clip mean square  ·  MSWithin = residual (within-clip) mean square

(You will spot the k − 1 in the denominator: k is simply the number of raters. We flag it now because it becomes the central lever from Part 2 onward — the "1" in ICC(C,1) means we are judging a single rater, and averaging over many raters, ICC(C,k), is what turns a modest single-rating ICC into a reliable measure.)

The numerator captures how much clips differ over and above random error; the denominator scales it by the total. Put simply, the larger the signal (the numerator), the more of the observed variance reflects the rank ordering we actually care about. The result runs from 0 (all noise) to 1 (all signal).

Applied to our data — a single rater scoring a single clip — the ICC comes out at 0.271: about 27% of the variance in a single rating is stable between-clip signal, the other 73% noise. This is exactly the split the Section 2 figure showed — the gentle climb of the consensus against the wide scatter of each rater's profile around it — now reduced to a single number.

What this means practically

The square root, √0.271 ≈ 0.52, is roughly how well one rater's score of one clip tracks that clip's consensus (47-rater) value. Moderate — and far from reliable enough to use as a measurement instrument.

How to read 0.271 — a starting point, not a verdict. By the thresholds often quoted (e.g., Koo & Li, 2016: >0.75 = good, 0.5–0.75 = moderate, <0.5 = poor) a single-rating ICC of 0.271 looks "poor" — but that misses the point. This is the signal density of one rater scoring one clip, not the reliability of the measure as we intend to use it. Concretely: in that single rating the signal we care about — the part that tracks the rank ordering of our 100 clips — is largely washed out by everything else — the raters' idiosyncratic clip-by-clip preferences and momentary noise. So to trust the ordering we recover, we cannot lean on single observations; we have to aggregate across many raters (and later across clips too). That is a natural intuition: any estimate is only as good as the number of observations that feed it. (And because the unit here is the clip, that signal is not even purely about the people yet — it mixes genuine identity differences with clip-to-clip differences within a person.) The build-up — and the reliable person-level score it produces — is the subject of Part 2.

4 — A note on what kind of ICC

The ICC family contains several distinct variants, and the differences matter. The two main distinctions are between one-way and two-way designs, and between absolute agreement and consistency.

A one-way design uses different raters for each target — each person is rated by a unique, non-overlapping group. A two-way design uses the same raters for all targets, so rater effects can be separated out as their own source of variance. Our study is two-way: all 47 raters scored all 20 identities.

Absolute agreement (ICC(A)) asks whether raters give the same absolute scores — if Rater A gives 6 and Rater B gives 4 to the same face, that disagreement counts against reliability. Consistency (ICC(C)) asks only whether raters rank the targets the same way — if Rater A gives 6 and Rater B gives 4 but they both put Face X above Face Y, that counts as consistent. Since our goal is rank ordering rather than absolute level, consistency is the appropriate variant we have selected (hence C in the ICC(C,1)!).

Throughout these materials we use the McGraw & Wong (1996) letter notation — ICC(C,1), ICC(C,k) — as it is more descriptive. The subscript after the comma refers to the number of raters: 1 for a single rater, k for the mean of k raters.

Why does the design (one-way vs two-way) change the ICC value?

In a one-way design, variance due to rater differences cannot be separated from other error — it all goes into the denominator. In a two-way crossed design (same raters for all targets), the rater main effect can be identified and, for the consistency ICC, removed from the denominator entirely. This is why ICC(C) is always at least as large as the equivalent one-way ICC: it treats scale-use differences between raters as irrelevant to the rank-ordering question, which is appropriate when that is genuinely our goal.

The practical implication: if you use a one-way ICC for data collected in a two-way design, you will get a lower (more conservative) reliability estimate than the consistency ICC. Both are technically valid — they answer slightly different questions about what counts as agreement.

5 — Where we are

A single rater scoring a single clip gives a rating that is about 27% signal and 73% noise, and the rank ordering implied by single ratings correlates only about 0.52 with the true underlying ordering. That is not good enough to build research on.

The Section 2 figure makes the reason intuitive: each rater's line wanders several points around the consensus from one clip to the next, so any single rater's score for a clip lands well off that clip's true value. Each rating is a single draw from the scores different raters give that clip, and one draw is a poor estimate of the clip's mean — exactly as a single observation drawn from a population is a noisy estimate of the population mean. You do not characterise a distribution with one data point, and the fix is the same here: take more draws, and as they accumulate the random fluctuations average out and the mean converges on the true value.

Crucially, 0.271 is not the reliability of our measure — it is the reliability of its smallest unit, one rater judging one clip. That single-rating value is fixed, but the reliability we can achieve is not: it depends on how many observations we average. So 0.271 says nothing about what averaging many will do — which is exactly where the story turns. The Spearman-Brown formula shows how the ICC grows as we aggregate across raters, and how many we need to reach a reliability fit for purpose. That is Part 2.
