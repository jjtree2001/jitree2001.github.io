The Reliability Problem — From Attenuation to Signal-to-Noise

A guide to measurement error in psychological research — tracing the history from Spearman's original insight through Classical Test Theory, to a modern signal-to-noise framework that reveals what reliability really is and what it demands of us.

Continues from Part B2 — Measuring What We Cannot See. Builds on the LMM machinery from Foundations Part 2 §04.

Section 01 · Foundations

Observed Scores, True Scores,
and a Century of Consequences

The previous page (B2 §A) was about how to improve the reliability of an individual's aggregate score by ensuring the items on a test "hang together" — high internal consistency, captured by Cronbach's α and its successors, gives us some confidence that the per-person sum reflects that person's true level on the latent construct T rather than item-specific noise. The IRT machinery in B2 Part B does related work from a different angle: rather than relying on the sum at all, it uses item-level models to estimate ability θ on a logit scale, and uses item and test information functions to flag where on the ability continuum the test gives precise estimates — and where it does not. Both lines of argument share an implicit deeper goal that B2 did not make fully explicit: we want the distribution of observed scores (whether sums or model-based θ estimates) across our sample of people to track the true distribution of T in the underlying population. A reliable test, in the strongest sense, is one whose observed between-person spread accurately reproduces the real between-person spread in the latent trait.

That gap — between the spread of observed scores in a sample and the true spread of T in the population — is the reliability problem this page takes up. The question is no longer "is each person's aggregate stable?" but "does the distribution of observed scores across people faithfully represent the distribution of true ability across the population?". The answer, it turns out, is "no, systematically and in one direction": observed individual differences are always attenuated relative to the truth, in proportion to how much measurement error sits between observed and true scores. Quantifying that attenuation, understanding what it does to every effect we estimate, and seeing what a modern signal-to-noise framework reveals that the classical reliability coefficients hide — that is the work of §02 onwards.

The reliability problem in this sense is not a recent discovery. It was identified in 1904 by Charles Spearman — in the very same paper that introduced the correlation coefficient to psychology. That Spearman recognised the measurement error problem at the moment he invented the tool most affected by it is one of the more elegant ironies in the history of science. The framework he proposed that year remains the foundation on which all subsequent work — including the most modern approaches — is built.

For readers arriving directly: the foundational machinery — Classical Test Theory, the X = T + e decomposition, Cronbach's α and its variants — is developed in detail in B2 §A. CTT also does not pin down whether the metric the observed score lives on is a meaningful one in the first place — that measurement-theoretic question is the subject of B1 — Three Fallacies of Psychological Measurement. The attenuation logic worked through below assumes both prior pieces have been settled.

Two ways to estimate reliability

Reliability — the proportion of observed score variance attributable to true score variance — can be estimated two broad ways: through internal consistency (how well items within a single administration agree, as indexed by Cronbach's α) or through test-retest reliability (correlating two administrations of the same measure). Both aim at the same underlying ratio σ²true / (σ²true + σ²error) but rely on different assumptions and can diverge in practice. B2 covered the first; this page is largely about the second, and §04 ultimately unifies them under the signal-to-noise framework.

One implication is worth flagging upfront because it shapes everything that follows: reliability is not just a property of a measure, but of a design. Tasks that maximise within-person effects often minimise between-person variance — a tension at the heart of modern reliability work that the historical arc below sets up.

2018 — HEDGE, POWELL & SUMNER

Show empirically that tasks producing robust group-level effects consistently yield poor test-retest reliability for individual differences. Attenuation is not a theoretical worry; it is pervasive and severe.
2024–2026 — ROUDER, MEHRVARZ & SCHNUERCH

Reframe reliability as γ² = σ²_B/σ²_W — a trial-size-invariant variance ratio independent of how many trials were run. From γ² alone you can derive the entire reliability curve as a function of trial size. Crucially, their 2026 paper argues that reliability itself is merely an intermediate quantity: the true goal is directly modelling uncertainty in correlations across tasks via hierarchical models, bypassing the reliability bottleneck entirely.
The Thread Running Through Everything

From Spearman to Rouder, the underlying idea is identical: observed scores mix signal and noise, and any statistic computed from them inherits that mixture in predictable ways. What has changed across the century is the precision with which we can quantify that mixture — and therefore the precision with which we can diagnose problems and prescribe remedies. The next section makes the consequences concrete, starting with Spearman's own attenuation formula and what it implies for the effects we report every day.

Section 02 · Attenuation

Attenuation: The Predictable
Deflation of Every Effect

Section 1 established that observed scores are a mixture of true score and error, and that reliability quantifies how much of the observed variance is signal. The natural next question is: what does this do to the statistics we compute from those scores? The answer — which Spearman derived in the same 1904 paper — is precise and sobering. Low reliability does not merely add imprecision; it introduces a systematic, one-directional bias that pushes every effect we measure toward zero. This bias is called attenuation, and it operates on correlations and group differences alike.

A note on notation: what is the ICC?

From here on, reliability is written as ICC — the intraclass correlation coefficient. The name comes from the way the correlation is computed: rather than correlating two distinct variables X and Y (one score per person on each, with each variable playing a clearly different role), the ICC correlates pairs of measurements that come from the same "class" — most commonly two administrations of the same test on the same person, where the labelling of "first" vs "second" measurement is arbitrary. Because there is no natural X / Y distinction, an inter-class correlation does not make sense; an intra-class one does.

Computationally, the ICC for a test-retest design is exactly the reliability ratio defined in §1: the proportion of total observed variance that is between-person true-score variance, σ²true / (σ²true + σ²error). A test with ICC = 0.80 means that 80% of the spread of observed scores across people reflects genuine differences in T, and 20% reflects measurement error. (There are several variants of the ICC that differ in how they treat raters, occasions, and one-way vs two-way models; for the present purposes the simplest reading — proportion of variance attributable to genuine between-person differences — is the one to keep in mind. The widgets below all use ICC in this sense.)

Attenuation of Correlations

When two measures are both imperfectly reliable, the observed correlation between them is systematically smaller than the true underlying correlation, in expectation. Spearman's attenuation formula gives the exact relationship:

r_observed = r_true × √(ICC_x × ICC_y)
Spearman's attenuation formula for correlations

The implication is stark. If two measures each have reliability of .5, and the true correlation between the underlying constructs is .8, the observed correlation will be .8 × √(.5 × .5) = .8 × .5 = .40 — exactly half the true value. A researcher who observes r = .40 and concludes the constructs are weakly related has been misled by measurement error, not by the constructs themselves.

Attenuation of Group Differences

A critical and often overlooked point: attenuation affects mean differences just as severely as it affects correlations. This is frequently misunderstood — researchers sometimes assume that poor reliability "only matters for individual differences research." This is wrong. Karvelis and Diaconescu (2025) demonstrate mathematically that observed standardised mean differences follow the same reliability-based scaling:

δ_observed = δ_true × √ICC
Attenuation formula for standardised group differences (Cohen's d), assuming equal reliability across groups

A true effect of d = 0.8 measured with an instrument of reliability .5 yields an observed d of 0.8 × √.5 ≈ 0.57. A "large" effect becomes a "medium" effect — not because the effect is smaller in reality, but because of how Cohen's d is constructed. Cohen's d is the mean difference between groups divided by the pooled standard deviation: d = (μ₁ − μ₂) / σ_pooled. Measurement error leaves the mean difference in the numerator unaffected — random noise cancels when averaged across participants. But that same noise inflates the pooled standard deviation in the denominator, making it larger than the true population SD. A larger denominator with an unchanged numerator always produces a smaller d. The effect is real and unchanged; the ruler has simply been made noisier, stretching the scale against which the difference is expressed.

Why Attenuation Is Always a Lower Bound — Never an Upper One

At this point a reasonable question arises: if measurement error is random, why does it always push effects downward? If errors are equally likely to be positive or negative, shouldn't they sometimes inflate an observed correlation and sometimes deflate it — producing a noisy estimate centred on the true value rather than a systematically lower one?

As we saw earlier in the resource, this is the intuition that is linked to the principle of the central limit theorem and so is correct when we talk about means — random error cancels across people, leaving group means unbiased. But a correlation is not a mean. It is a ratio of covariance to variance, and error affects the numerator and denominator very differently:

r = Covariance(X, Y) / √(Variance(X) × Variance(Y))
The correlation as a ratio — and why each component is affected differently by error

When you add random error to both measures, the covariance in the numerator is unchanged — provided the errors on measure X and the errors on measure Y are independent of each other. But those same errors inflate the variance in the denominator — because each person's observed score now fluctuates around their true score, making both distributions wider. A larger denominator with the same numerator always means a smaller fraction. Always.

Attenuation and Regression to the Mean — The Same Phenomenon

This connects to a concept that often surprises students: attenuation and regression to the mean (RTM) are the same underlying mathematical fact, viewed from different angles.

RTM describes what happens to a single person across two measurement occasions. If someone scores extremely high on a first measurement, their score on retest tends to be lower — closer to the group mean. The reason is identical to the attenuation argument: their extreme first score is partly driven by error. On retest that error is not repeated, so the expected score shifts back toward the mean. The extreme score regresses toward the mean in proportion to how much of its variance was error — that proportion is precisely 1 − reliability.

RTM and Attenuation — One Idea, Two Views

RTM (within-person, across occasions): A person's extreme observed score will likely be less extreme on retest — because the error that pushed it to the extreme is random and will not repeat. The score regresses toward the group mean in proportion to measurement error.

Attenuation (between-persons, across measures): An observed correlation between two imperfect measures is always smaller than the true correlation — because error inflates variance without inflating covariance. The correlation regresses toward zero in proportion to measurement error.

Both follow directly from the same source: the correlation between an observed score and the true score it represents is √reliability — less than 1.0 whenever measurement is imperfect.

Critical Point

Attenuation does not diminish as sample size increases. Adding more participants makes you increasingly confident in a badly attenuated value — not in the true value. The only remedy is better measurement (higher reliability), not larger samples. This asymptotic property of attenuation is one of the most important and underappreciated facts in psychological methodology.

Section 03 · Dichotomisation

The Hidden Cost of Cutting
Continuous Measures

Many researchers in psychology and neuroscience routinely convert continuous measures into groups — median splits into "high" and "low" scorers, clinical thresholds creating "patient" versus "control" groups, or factorial designs that require categorical independent variables. This practice introduces a further layer of distortion that compounds the attenuation problem described above.

Why Observed Group Differences Depend on Where You Cut

When you dichotomise a continuous distribution, the observed standardised difference between your two groups is not simply a function of the true underlying difference. It depends on two things working together: the overall variance of the underlying distribution, and where you place the cut point.

The Misclassification Problem

Add measurement error and the situation worsens further. When the underlying measure is unreliable, individuals near the cut point are frequently misclassified — a true "high" scorer falls below the median on this occasion, and vice versa. The consequence is that attenuation compounds: the observed group difference is attenuated first by measurement error in the underlying measure (following δ_observed = δ_true × √ICC) and then further by the information discarded through dichotomisation itself.

Connection to ANOVA Designs

The pressure toward dichotomisation often comes from the desire to use factorial ANOVA designs, which require categorical independent variables. As a rule, any analysis that uses a continuous measure as a categorical independent variable is throwing away information and inflating required sample sizes. Regression and mixed models with continuous predictors are almost always preferable.

The practical consequence is that researchers using dichotomised designs need substantially larger sample sizes to achieve the same statistical power as those using continuous analyses — often two to three times larger, depending on reliability and cut location. The two sources of information loss are multiplicative, not additive.

Section 04 · Advanced

A Better Measure of Reliability:
The Signal-to-Noise Ratio Advanced

The previous sections showed that reliability determines the degree of attenuation in both correlations and group differences. But there is a deeper problem with how reliability is conventionally measured that limits our ability to diagnose and fix the problem. Reliability has always been a signal-to-noise ratio at heart — the classical definition ρ = σ²_T / (σ²_T + σ²_E) is exactly that. The genuine novelty of the framework introduced in this section — due to Rouder and Mehrvarz (2024, 2026) — is not that it reframes reliability as SNR but that it exposes the trial-size-invariant version of the SNR, separating the task's intrinsic signal-to-noise property from the implementation-dependent reliability coefficient that classical α and ICC actually return.

The Problem with Conventional Reliability Coefficients

That α depends on how many items you administer was the central message of B2 §A — large k with positive r̄ pushes α toward 1 by aggregation; low k with the same r̄ leaves α much further from 1. The less-obvious point worth making explicit here is that test-retest ICC has the same dependence. Test-retest correlates each person's summed (or averaged) score at time 1 with their summed score at time 2, and both summed scores are noisy estimates of the same underlying true ability — the precision of those estimates depends on how many trials or items you collected on each occasion. More trials → sharper per-person estimates → higher correlation between time-1 and time-2 scores. The aggregation logic Cronbach uses to produce a reliable composite within a single administration is, one level up, exactly what produces a reliable test-retest correlation across two administrations: the rank-ordering of people by summed score is more stable when each person's summed score is itself a more stable estimate of their true ability. So both α and ICC are L-dependent, and for the same underlying reason.

Consider two labs both studying the Stroop task. Lab A runs 200 trials per person per condition; Lab B runs 20 trials per person per condition. Lab A will report much higher test-retest reliability than Lab B — but the task is identical. The reliability coefficient has told us something about the experimental implementation, not about the task itself.

The Signal-to-Noise Ratio γ²

Rouder and Mehrvarz propose a variance ratio defined at the trial level rather than the score level:

γ² = σ²_B / σ²_W
Between-person true variance (signal) divided by within-person per-trial noise

σ²B is the genuine variance in people's true ability — how much people actually differ from one another. σ²W is the per-trial noise — the variance of a single trial-level observation around the person's true value. The crucial property of σ²W is that aggregating trials does not reduce it: collecting more trials reduces the variance of the mean (by σ²W/L), but the per-trial noise itself is a property of the task and the participant, unchanged by implementation. That is what makes γ² trial-size-invariant where classical α and ICC are not — they measure the precision of the aggregate (which depends on L), while γ² measures the underlying SNR at the level of a single trial (which doesn't). The reliability you actually measure is what falls out of γ² plus your L choice; the formula on the next line is precisely that derivation.

From γ², the expected test-retest reliability at any trial size L follows directly:

E(r) ≈ γ² / (γ² + 2/L)
The reliability curve — reliability as a function of γ² and trial size L
Where does the 2 in 2/L come from?

Worth pausing on. Rouder's framework is built around the kind of design that dominates cognitive psychology: a within-subject experimental contrast (Stroop = incongruent − congruent reaction time, flanker effect, congruency effect, etc.). On any given occasion, a person's score is the difference between two condition means. If each condition has L trials, the variance of each condition mean is σ²_W / L. The variance of the difference of two independent means is the sum of their variances — so 2σ²_W / L. That is the noise term in the test-retest formula's denominator: σ²_B + 2σ²_W / L. Pulling σ²_W out of the ratio (so it appears as 2/L next to γ²) is just a rearrangement. The factor of 2 is therefore specific to two-condition contrast designs; for a single-condition design where the score is just one mean, the factor would be 1.

This formula defines an entire curve of reliability as a function of trial size — and the shape of that curve is completely determined by γ². A task with high γ² rises steeply: reasonable reliability is achieved with modest trial counts. A task with low γ² rises slowly and may never reach acceptable reliability within any practical design.

This is structurally the same problem we have already met, twice, at different levels. Part 4 §5 — Power analysis showed that small Cohen's d requires more participants to detect cleanly: required N scales roughly as 1/d². B2 §A — The aggregation principle showed that small Cronbach r̄ requires more items to reach a target α. Now we see that small γ² requires more trials L to reach a target test-retest reliability. (In experimental psychology "item" and "trial" are often used interchangeably — a self-report scale and a Stroop block both involve many within-participant observations being aggregated — so r̄ and γ² differ on framing rather than level.) Three per-unit signal-to-noise quantities, one structural fact: when the per-unit signal is small, more units have to be aggregated, and the aggregation requirement scales with the inverse of the squared SNR. Rouder's framework makes that requirement explicit at the trial level, and his 2026 paper goes further and argues that hierarchical models let you bypass the reliability bottleneck entirely — the topic of the LMM bridge below.

Bridging to Linear Mixed Models

LMMs are not new to this resource. They were developed in detail in Foundations Part 2 §03 — Repeated measures, structured dependence and §04 — Linear Mixed Models: modelling the dependence structure, where we saw how a single likelihood with random effects for participants (and optionally items) can replace ANOVA's blunter machinery, partition variance into between-person and residual components, and stabilise individual estimates via partial pooling. They were also flagged in B1 §5 as one of the more honest responses to the measurement problems classical CTT papers tend to leave unaddressed. The bridge built here is a specific one — between Rouder's variance-ratio reliability index and the variance components a fitted LMM gives you for free — and it is what makes Rouder's index practically actionable rather than just conceptually cleaner. Rouder's data model is:

Yikℓ ~ Normal(αi + xkθi, τ²)
Rouder's model: person effect, condition contrast, and trial noise — no item subscript

Reading the symbols out: Yikℓ is the observation on trial ℓ for person i in condition k. αi is the person-specific intercept — person i's overall level on the task (their baseline reaction time, their average accuracy, etc.). xk is a contrast indicator that codes the experimental condition (typically +1/−1 for the two conditions of a within-subject contrast — e.g. +1 for incongruent, −1 for congruent in a Stroop design). θi is the person-specific contrast effect — how much person i is slowed by incongruent trials, or whatever the experimental contrast is, treated as an individual-differences quantity. τ² is the within-person trial variance — the per-trial noise σ²W that appears in γ²'s denominator. In plain words: each observed trial-level response is the person's baseline (αi), plus the person's individual contrast effect for that condition (xkθi), plus Gaussian trial noise with variance τ². In LMM vocabulary, the two person-indexed quantities αi and θi are exactly what a model with a random intercept and a random slope for the condition contrast estimates — one slope per person, drawn from a between-person distribution whose variance σ²B is the numerator of γ².

Notice there is no item subscript anywhere in this model. The index ℓ runs over replicate trials, and τ² is simply the variance of each trial around the person-by-condition mean. Items do not exist as a modelled entity — they are absorbed entirely into τ². This is a deliberate departure from the standard LMM treatment developed in Part 2 §04, where random effects are routinely included for items as well as for participants — random intercepts for item difficulty, and where appropriate random slopes for how strongly each item taps the construct. The crossed LMM in the next equation is exactly that fuller picture. Rouder's model collapses all of the item-level structure into τ² — not an oversight but a simplification appropriate for the paradigms he considers (Stroop, flanker), where trials are effectively independent random draws from a large pool of nominally exchangeable stimuli, and the derivation of the test-retest reliability formula requires that noise on Day 1 and Day 2 be statistically independent. Random item sampling across occasions is the assumption baked into the whole framework.

ypi = μ + up + vi + εpi
Crossed LMM: between-person variance (σ²person), item intercept variance (σ²item), and residual trial noise (σ²residual)

What the LMM bridge actually adds

The LMM gives you separate variance components for person, item, and residual. Two distinct things this enables, depending on whether Rouder's design assumptions hold:

(a) Diagnostic on τ² — when Rouder's framework applies. When trials are genuinely exchangeable random draws (Stroop, flanker, RT contrasts — the cognitive paradigms Rouder targets), Rouder's τ² ≈ σ²residual + σ²item-sampling, and the LMM gives you the same reliability estimate as the τ²-based formula. What the LMM adds in this case is not a different number but a decomposition: τ² is one bucket, and the LMM tells you whether what's inside is mostly residual trial noise (intrinsic to the task — hard to fix without redesign) or mostly item-level variance (potentially fixable by curating better items). Useful for asking why a task has low SNR; redundant for the reliability number itself.

(b) The right tool — when Rouder's framework does not apply. When items are fixed across occasions (a questionnaire reused on two days, the same word list, the same image set), σ²item is shared structure that cancels from the test-retest covariance. Rouder's τ² lumps σ²item into the noise denominator anyway and would underestimate reliability. The LMM, by separating σ²item from σ²residual, lets you correctly treat σ²item as shared structure rather than noise. Here the LMM is not a refinement of Rouder's framework but a correction — the right tool because Rouder's is the wrong one.

The deeper point is that the design question is not "which formula to pick within Rouder's framework" — it is whether Rouder's framework applies at all. For exchangeable-trial designs (cognitive paradigms with random stimuli): yes, with the LMM as a diagnostic supplement. For fixed-item designs (questionnaires reused across occasions): no, and the LMM is the alternative tool that gets the answer right. There is no design-type flag the analyst sets — the data structure imposes the choice via whether the same item identifiers reappear across occasions, and the analyst's job is to recognise which case they are in and read the variance components accordingly.

A Worked Example: Face Recognition with a 2×2 Design

To make this concrete, consider a 100-item face recognition memory task with a 2×2 design: Factor A = Upright vs Inverted, Factor B = Familiar vs Unfamiliar. Two hundred participants each complete all conditions. You fit a crossed hierarchical model with participant and item random slopes for both factors and their interaction:

accuracy ~ A * B + (1 + A*B | participant) + (1 + A*B | item)
Full crossed model — random slopes for both participants and items across all effects

This example makes a point that a single reliability coefficient would completely obscure: the same task is simultaneously excellent for measuring overall ability, mediocre for inversion sensitivity, and essentially unsuitable for studying individual differences in the interaction effect. A researcher who runs 100 items and reports a high reliability for the overall memory score has no grounds for assuming the inversion contrast is equally well measured — yet this assumption is routinely made in the literature.

Consulting the reliability curve for each γ²: overall ability (γ² = 0.50) achieves r ≈ .96 at L = 100 trials, meaning very few additional items are needed. The inversion effect (γ² = 0.15) achieves only r ≈ .88 — still useful, but considerably more fragile. The interaction (γ² = 0.038) achieves r ≈ .66 — below the conventional threshold of .70 even with 100 trials per condition, and would require over 450 trials to reach .90. No amount of modest sample-size increase rescues this; only more items or a fundamentally different task design will help.

Item Design Is a Reliability Design Decision

Two further lessons from this example. First: the choice of fixed versus sampled items matters. Using the same 100 faces on both occasions was what made item intercept variance "free" — if different face sets were drawn each time, item-sampling variance would enter the noise denominator and every γ² would drop. Second: low SNR may reflect not just too few items but poorly discriminating ones. If the inversion effect shows low person slope variance (σ²p-A = 0.12), the task structure itself limits what can be recovered — brute-force item addition is less effective than redesigning items to amplify between-person differences in the contrast of interest.

Why This Matters: The Curve Reveals What the Coefficient Hides

The reliability curve makes visible something that a single ICC value completely conceals: the shape of the curve — and therefore exactly how many trials are needed to reach any reliability target. Consider the Stroop effect (γ ≈ .12). Plugging into the formula, achieving r = .70 requires approximately 158 trials per condition; achieving r = .90 requires over 600. Most studies run 20–50 trials per condition. No amount of increased sample size changes this — the ceiling is set by γ², not by N.

Contrast this with the Poggendorf visual illusion (γ ≈ 1.41). With just 15 trials per person, test-retest reliability exceeds .90. The task is intrinsically high-signal: individual differences in susceptibility to the illusion are large relative to trial noise. The individual differences research programme built on this task is on solid ground regardless of how few trials are used.

The Connection Back to Attenuation

The full circle closes here. Recall from Section 2 that attenuation of correlations follows r_observed = r_true × √(ICC_x × ICC_y). The ICC values in that formula are themselves determined by γ² and L. So for any task in any study, the degree of attenuation in reported correlations is:

r_observed ≈ r_true × γ²/(γ² + 2/L)
Attenuation as a direct function of the task's signal-to-noise ratio and trial size

Importantly, this also means that for contrast-based paradigms — such as the well documented face inversion effect — observed γ will also be characteristically low, because the subtraction (i.e., upright − inverted) cancels much of the between-person variance while doubling the noise. As a result, poor cross-task correlations across different inversion tasks or paradigms are not simply evidence of construct fragmentation. They are the mathematically predictable consequence of attempting to localise individuals in a distribution using a ruler too noisy to distinguish nearby positions. In signal-to-noise terms, low γ² directly constrains the maximum observable reliability, and therefore the upper bound on any cross-task association.

Beyond Reliability: The Real Endpoint Is Correlation Uncertainty

Rouder's 2026 paper (with Mehrvarz and Schnuerch) makes a striking further argument: once hierarchical models are available, reliability is not even the right endpoint — it is merely an intermediate quantity, useful for planning but not for interpretation.

The core problem is that conventional sample correlations between contrast scores are not only attenuated — they are inconsistent estimators. Adding more participants makes you increasingly certain of a wrong value. The confidence interval around a sample correlation reflects only the uncertainty from a finite number of individuals, completely ignoring the uncertainty from a finite number of trials. As trial count drops, this omitted uncertainty grows — but the CI stays the same width, creating false precision.

The Right Target: Uncertainty on Correlations Directly

Rouder et al. (2026) advocate modelling trial noise explicitly via a bivariate hierarchical model, which simultaneously estimates the true between-task correlation and provides credible intervals that correctly incorporate both sources of uncertainty — finite individuals and finite trials. The resulting intervals are typically 3–4× wider than conventional CIs, reflecting the true state of knowledge. Disattenuation emerges naturally from the model rather than as a post-hoc correction. The old adage that you cannot correlate unreliable instruments is wrong — you can, so long as uncertainty is appropriately expressed.

In this view, γ² remains valuable for planning — it tells you how many trials are needed to achieve a target width for your posterior credible interval on the correlation. But the reliability coefficient itself becomes tangential for interpretation. Report uncertainty directly; let the hierarchical model do the disattenuation implicitly. This is a meaningful shift in emphasis from the earlier Rouder and Mehrvarz (2024) framing, and it connects directly to the argument in this resource: the chain of attenuation is quantifiable at every link, and the remedy is modelling, not just more trials or larger samples.

Summary: The Full Chain of Measurement Error

Poor γ² → low reliability at any practical trial count → attenuated and inconsistently estimated observed correlations → underestimation of true effect sizes → misleading conclusions about construct structure, test validity, and theoretical models. Every link in this chain is quantifiable. The remedy is not more participants — it is either more trials, better task design (higher γ²), or statistical modelling that explicitly partitions trial noise from true ability variance and reports uncertainty accordingly.
