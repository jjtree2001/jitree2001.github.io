Rating Reliability: the Mila project

A walkthrough of reliability in a friendliness-rating study — 47 raters, 100 clips, 20 identities — from the intraclass correlation through to generalisability theory, and the design-planning document it fed into.

Part 1
What ICC Is and What It Tells Us
Signal and noise inside a single rating. ICC(C,1) = 0.271 — roughly 27% signal — and why the consistency variant is the right one for a rank-ordering question.
Read Part 1 →
Part 2
What Aggregation Does
Averaging across raters and clips lifts the ICC from 0.271 to 0.946 via Spearman–Brown — but that is reliability for these materials, not yet for new ones.
Read Part 2 →
Part 3
What a Mixed Model Reveals
Variance decomposed into named sources — identity, clip, rater generosity, idiosyncratic taste. Aggregation suppresses these; the model explains them.
Read Part 3 →
Part 4
From Reliability to Generalisability
Three kinds of reliability, Cronbach's G-theory and Rouder & Mehrvarz's signal-to-noise framework — turning an internal consistency check into a design question.

Planning document
Identity Signal Reliability Planning
A Bayesian cumulative-logit decomposition of the ratings into identity signal and its competing noise sources, then reliability across candidate designs. Moving from 47 raters × 5 images to 50 × 10 takes absolute reliability from ≈.735 to ≈.820 — and adding images beats adding raters, because image-sampling noise is the least-averaged component.

Single case

Interpreting an Individual Score

What it actually takes to call one person's test score abnormal — and how much of the answer is decided by the framework rather than the data.

Analysis
Is That Score Really Extreme? From z-Scores to Bayes
Conventional z-scores answer "how surprising is this?", not the clinical question. Crawford's frequentist corrections handle measurement error; Bayesian approaches ask about group membership and true-score estimation directly, with base rates and reliability doing the work. Run through on developmental prosopagnosia, where identical data reach different conclusions depending on the framework.
