Self-reported and objective face recognition: testing general and face-specific components

A large-sample observation suggests that self-reported face recognition contains both a general recognition-memory signal and a face-specific contribution. The proposed study tests that account with better measures and an item-level model.

1. The data

Jeremy Wilmer provided data from 3,574 participants who completed three measures:

  • the Cambridge Face Memory Test (CFMT), an objective test of face recognition with 72 correct-or-incorrect trials;
  • the Cambridge Face Memory Questionnaire (CFMQ), 18 self-report items about one's own face recognition;
  • a short everyday non-face recognition questionnaire, with five self-report items about recognising non-face stimuli such as luggage, pets, streets, houses and cars.

The CFMT is a performance measure; the other two are self-reports. The central question is not only how well the questionnaires predict performance, but what produces that prediction. Reliability matters here: internal consistency is high for both the CFMQ (.90) and the CFMT (.90), but low for the five-item non-face questionnaire (.57).

The first correlation is expected: people have some insight into their own face recognition, so their self-ratings track their objective scores. The second is more surprising. The non-face questionnaire contains no face items, yet it predicts CFMT performance—weakly, but statistically significantly. Ratings of one's ability to recognise luggage, cars and other non-face stimuli therefore carry some information about face-recognition performance.

3. What the observation implies

Because the non-face questionnaire and the CFMT share no content, their association is unlikely to be face-specific. It may reflect general recognition ability, a broad self-belief about recognition, or genuine insight that extends across recognition-memory domains. The present data cannot distinguish these explanations. They do, however, show two important things.

The CFMQ appears to contain more than one signal

Two results matter. The non-face questionnaire correlates strongly with the CFMQ (corrected r = .71) but only weakly with the CFMT (corrected r = .21). A single common factor with independent residuals cannot reproduce this pattern; formally, it would require a CFMQ loading greater than one. At least one additional source of association is therefore needed. A plausible account is that the CFMQ contains a general component shared with other recognition self-ratings and a face-specific component that strengthens its relation with face performance. The three existing measures do not establish those labels uniquely, however. Response style, overlapping item content or another unmeasured trait may also contribute. The new study tests the two-component account rather than assuming it.

The cross-domain signal is poorly measured

The cross-domain association currently depends on a five-item questionnaire with reliability of only .57. Its correlation with the CFMT is therefore imprecise; plausible values run from about .10 to .35. A longer, broader non-face questionnaire should increase precision and reduce the chance that the result depends on a narrow set of non-face categories. Better measurement is a prerequisite for asking how large the general component is and what may explain it.

4. Building a better non-face questionnaire

The new questionnaire should match the CFMQ in length, response format and general construction while replacing face content with non-face recognition content. Matching the instruments in these respects makes content, rather than measurement design, the main difference between them.

The items would come from three sources. First, existing CFMQ items would be recast in non-face form: for example, “I can recognise a friend from a photograph” might become “I can recognise my own car among similar cars.” Second, the five existing non-face items—covering luggage, pets, houses and cars—would be developed into fuller sets. Third, new items would broaden the range of everyday recognition categories.

Two issues require care. Items about streets, houses and routes may tap navigation rather than object recognition and should be examined separately. Positive and negative wording should also be balanced across the two questionnaires so that response style operates comparably. A development pool of 35–40 items could be piloted in an independent sample. About 20 would then be selected using content coverage, response distributions, factor structure, item–total relations and checks for redundancy. The final scale should be evaluated in a separate validation sample, with reliability comparable to the CFMQ but not achieved by retaining near-duplicate items.

5. The proposed study

Once developed, the new questionnaire would be administered with the CFMQ, CFMT and AAMT. Together, these measures allow the study to test whether a common cross-domain self-assessment signal predicts objective recognition memory and to estimate the additional face-specific contribution.

An objective non-face test: the Abstract Art Memory Test

The Abstract Art Memory Test (AAMT) is an objective recognition-memory test that uses the same three-alternative forced-choice format as the CFMT but substitutes abstract art for faces. In a separate sample of 1,471 participants, its reliability was about .80 (see Appendix).

The AAMT is not intended as a domain-matched criterion for the non-face questionnaire: the questionnaire concerns broad everyday recognition, not abstract art. Its value is precisely that it provides objective non-face recognition performance in a distinct content domain. The design therefore yields two cross-domain paths: non-face questionnaire → CFMT and CFMQ → AAMT. If the same latent self-assessment component accounts for both, that would support a general recognition-memory insight signal more strongly than either path alone. These paths are not pure measures of insight, however; general ability, broad self-beliefs or other shared influences may also contribute.

The AAMT also helps isolate face-specific validity. After accounting for measurement error and the covariance between the CFMT and AAMT, any additional path from the face-specific CFMQ component to the CFMT would be consistent with face-specific insight. This is not equivalent to subtracting one raw correlation from another, because the tasks differ psychometrically and the general component may relate differently to each.

The conceptual model

A more reliable non-face questionnaire may overlap substantially with the CFMQ. The key question is how much of that overlap is tied to objective performance. Figure 1 represents a candidate general self-assessment component that predicts both objective tasks, alongside residual questionnaire covariance. The residual may include method variance, response style, broad self-beliefs, unmeasured content overlap or model misspecification; the design cannot identify it uniquely as method.

The item-level statistical model

Figure 1 is conceptual. The statistical analysis should use item and trial responses rather than four summed scores. The CFMQ and non-face-questionnaire items would define latent self-report dimensions; the CFMT and AAMT trials would define separate latent performance dimensions through categorical or item-response models. Item-level modelling separates construct variance from item-specific error and avoids treating reliability coefficients as fixed and known.

Figure 2 shows a natural starting point: a bifactor-style model for the questionnaire items. All face and non-face items would load on a general recognition self-assessment factor, and each item would also load on a domain-specific factor — face-specific for the CFMQ items, non-face-specific for the non-face items — that captures the content-specific variance left over once the general factor is removed. The two objective performance factors, CFMT and AAMT, would be allowed to correlate. The general self-assessment factor would then predict both objective factors; these two validity paths define the candidate general recognition-memory insight signal. The face-specific factor would additionally predict the CFMT, estimating the contribution unique to face self-assessment. The non-face-specific factor carries no validity path. It is the covariance among the non-face items left once the general factor is removed — the part of self-rated non-face recognition that does not generalise to face self-ratings. That residual could be genuine domain-specific self-insight, a domain-specific self-belief with no basis in ability, or mere clustering among similar items; this design cannot separate them, since testing it would need an objective test of everyday non-face recognition matched to the questionnaire, which the AAMT (abstract art) is not (and it's not obvious what such a test would actually comprise!). It therefore serves only to keep the general factor clean and is not interpreted substantively.

The model itself is well specified; what determines whether its parts can be estimated is the strength of the measures feeding it, and this is exactly what the new design improves. The general and face-specific components separate only when the general factor is well anchored — that is, when it loads on two reliable questionnaires and predicts two objective tasks. The existing data do not meet this condition: a single five-item non-face questionnaire is too weak an anchor, so on those data the general and face-specific parts trade off against each other while their combined effect on the CFMT stays fixed, and only the total validity is precisely estimated. The new design removes that limitation directly, by adding a longer, more reliable non-face questionnaire and a second objective test. This is the main reason the study is worth running: the same model that can only estimate the total on the current data can estimate its decomposition once these measures are in place. That the decomposition recovers cleanly at the planned sample size and test lengths can be confirmed in advance by a parameter-recovery simulation — data generated from known values and re-estimated — before the real data are fitted. Identification also rests on transparent choices about factor scaling, whether the general and domain-specific factors are orthogonal, wording effects and the residual relation between the CFMT and AAMT. The model should be compared with alternatives, including one with no common cross-domain effect and one with two unrelated cross-domain paths. A general factor earns the label insight only if it predicts both objective factors; questionnaire covariance alone shows only general self-assessment.

What the study can establish

If the general questionnaire factor explains little variance in the objective tasks, the questionnaires may share substantial variance without much objective validity, and the CFMQ's validity would be largely face-specific. If the factor predicts both tasks meaningfully, the evidence for general recognition-memory insight would be stronger. Any remaining questionnaire covariance should be reported as unexplained self-report overlap, not pure method variance.

One possible explanation for a general signal is general recognition ability. The CFMT and AAMT correlate at about .31 at the latent level (see Appendix). The model can test whether the cross-domain questionnaire paths remain after accounting for this objective covariance. If they do, shared ability is not the whole explanation; if they do not, the result would be consistent with an ability account. Even then, two objective tasks cannot separate broad recognition ability from shared task format, and any residual signal could reflect metacognitive insight, personality or another unmeasured trait.

The study therefore has two principal outputs. First, it tests a candidate latent general recognition-memory insight component through the complementary cross-domain paths from the non-face questionnaire to the CFMT and from the CFMQ to the AAMT. Second, it estimates the additional face-specific relation between the CFMQ and the CFMT, while separating objectively predictive self-assessment from residual questionnaire covariance. Using item-level data is central to both aims.

Appendix. Divergent validity of the Abstract Art Memory Test

The AAMT serves as the objective non-face criterion. This appendix summarises evidence for that role from 1,471 participants who completed both the 50-item AAMT and the 72-item CFMT in the same three-alternative forced-choice format.

Both tasks are reliable enough for individual-differences research. The CFMT is the stronger instrument: it has higher reliability, roughly twice the median item discrimination and better precision across the ability range. The AAMT is sound but less discriminating, especially among high performers. This is a ceiling in measurement precision, not in raw scores; neither test is at floor or ceiling.

The summed scores correlate modestly (Pearson r = .26). A correlated two-factor item-response model yields a latent correlation of .31, closely matching the disattenuated correlation implied by the two reliabilities. A one-factor model is decisively worse (ΔBIC ≈ 3,020), and a guessing-aware model with the lower asymptote fixed at one third gives nearly the same latent correlation (.32). The tasks therefore share only about one tenth of their latent variance.

With only two tasks, that shared variance cannot be divided between general ability and common task format. The .31 should therefore be treated as shared latent variance of uncertain origin. This does not undermine the AAMT's role as a divergent non-face criterion. Its relation with the CFMT is close to published face–car correlations (.26–.28) and far below correlations between two measures of the same ability (.61–.79). The AAMT therefore provides a reliable but clearly distinct non-face recognition measure.
